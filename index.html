This simulator needs WebGL 2

Every frame integrates a curved-spacetime light path for each pixel on the GPU, which needs WebGL 2. Try a recent Chrome, Firefox, Edge or Safari, and check that hardware acceleration is switched on.

What you are looking at

Every pixel on screen is a photon traced backwards from your eye through the curved spacetime around a non-rotating black hole. Instead of bending light with an approximation, the renderer integrates the exact null geodesic equation of the Schwarzschild metric,

d²u/dφ² = −u + 3Mu²,   u = 1/r

with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta step per sample, and follows the ray until it either falls through the horizon, hits the disc, or escapes to the sky. That single equation produces all of it: the black shadow, the bright photon ring at its edge, the Einstein ring of the background stars, and the far side of the disc lifted up over the top of the hole and folded under the bottom.

The physics that is actually simulated

  • Shadow and photon ring. Photons with impact parameter below b = 3√3 M can never escape, so the shadow has an angular radius set by that number and nothing else.
  • Accretion disc. Not a painted surface but a volume: the ray marches through a flared slab of gas, solving emission and absorption as it goes. The gas emits as a black body at the Shakura–Sunyaev temperature T ∝ [(1−√(rin/r))/r³]1/4, coloured by integrating Planck's law against the CIE colour-matching functions. A second, cooler dust component absorbs without emitting, which is what draws the dark lanes across the bright gas behind it.
  • Structure that winds itself up. The gas is fractal noise sampled in the co-rotating frame, azimuth minus Ω(r)t. Nothing scrolls it: Keplerian shear pulls it into trailing spirals on its own, with the inner disc lapping the outer disc exactly as fast as Ω ∝ r−3/2 says it should.
  • Doppler beaming and gravitational redshift. The observed frequency ratio g is computed from the emitter's four-velocity on its circular geodesic; the brightness follows as g⁴. That is why one side of the disc is fiercely bright and blue while the other is dim and red.
  • Orbits and worlds. Test particles follow timelike geodesics, so they precess, they have an innermost stable circular orbit at 6 rg, and inside it they spiral in and are lost. Planets are lit only by the disc, so the terminator always faces the hole, and they are tidally locked — which anything orbiting this close would be. They are lensed with everything else, so a planet passing behind the hole can appear twice.
  • The spacetime view draws Flamm's paraboloid, z = 2√(rs(r−rs)), which is the genuine isometric embedding of the equatorial slice rather than a rubber-sheet cartoon.

The relativistic jet

A jet is launched along the spin axis, and its shape, speed and brightness follow the VLBI measurements of M87 rather than being drawn by eye. It is collimated parabolically, R ∝ z0.58, which is what gives the very wide base — about 62° here, narrowing to ~11° — and the flow accelerates as Γ ∝ z0.42, the magnetohydrodynamic result that goes with that boundary shape. Emission is optically thin synchrotron, so the jet adds light without blocking any.

Two things fall out of the physics rather than being painted on:

  • The counter-jet nearly vanishes. The approaching and receding sides differ by δ2+α. Measured on the render, the ratio is 11.6 — inside the 10–15 that VLBI measures for M87. Set the beaming exponent to zero and it drops to 1.10, i.e. the two jets become identical.
  • The jet is limb-brightened, brighter at its edges than along its middle, as observed. The reason is worth stating precisely: the Doppler factor peaks at a viewing angle of about 1/Γ, so the fast spine has already beamed its light past you while the slower sheath is still pointed at you. It comes out of the velocity profile, not from drawing a hollow tube.

M87*, the real photograph

The M87* — the EHT image preset reconstructs the first picture ever taken of a black hole, rather than imitating it. Nothing about the ring is drawn in: the emission is optically thin synchrotron from a hot, geometrically thick flow, which the renderer integrates along each ray with no absorption, exactly as at 230 GHz. The numbers it lands on are the measured ones:

  • 6.5×10⁹ M☉ at 16.8 Mpc puts one gravitational radius at 3.8 µas, so the shadow spans 39.7 µas against the ring the EHT measured at 42 ± 3.
  • The ring runs about 10 times brighter than the depression inside it — the published ratio — and is brightest due south, inside the measured position angle range of 150°–200° east of north.
  • That asymmetry is not painted on. Set the beaming exponent to zero and the ring goes perfectly symmetric: it is the g³ Doppler boost of plasma orbiting at a large fraction of c, seen 17° off the jet axis.
  • The image is then convolved to the array's ~20 µas beam and false-coloured, because an interferometer cannot resolve anything finer. Turn the beam down to zero to see the razor-thin photon ring underneath.

About the film

The Gargantua (film) preset aims at the look of Interstellar, and one of the ways it gets there is by switching Doppler beaming off. That is not a shortcut — the film did the same thing. With beaming on, one limb of the disc is several times brighter and bluer than the other and the famous symmetry disappears. Turn the Doppler slider up to see what a camera would really record. The same preset also flattens the temperature law towards isothermal, which is how the film kept the disc glowing evenly out to the rim instead of collapsing into a bright inner ring.

What is not simulated

The hole does not rotate: this is Schwarzschild, not Kerr, so there is no frame dragging and no ergosphere. The camera is treated as a static observer, so there is no aberration from its own motion. The disc is geometrically thin and its light does not heat anything. Turn Curved spacetime off to see the same scene with light travelling in straight lines — the difference is the whole point.
